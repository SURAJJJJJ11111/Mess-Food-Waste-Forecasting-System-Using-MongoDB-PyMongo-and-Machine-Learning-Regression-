SLIDE 4: WHY MONGODB? NOSQL ADVANTAGES OVER RDBMS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[Visual Layout]:
- 2-Column Comparison Table: [Traditional Relational SQL] vs [MongoDB NoSQL Solution]

[Slide Content]:
1. Dynamic Itemized Menus (1:Few Relationship):
   - SQL: Requires 3 normalized tables (Meals, Menu_Items, Dishes) with expensive multi-table JOINs.
   - MongoDB: Embeds `menu_items` array directly in `meal_records`. Zero join latency; O(1) single-document read.

2. Document-Level Atomicity:
   - SQL: Requires complex multi-table ACID transaction locks for daily meal updates.
   - MongoDB: Single atomic document write guarantees consistency across all items in one operation.

3. Server-Side Aggregation Power:
   - SQL: Heavy data processing requires pulling thousands of rows into Python memory.
   - MongoDB: Native C++ Aggregation Pipeline executes grouping, unwinding, and math directly on the database engine.

4. BSON Serialization:
   - Extends standard JSON with native UTC timestamps, 64-bit IEEE doubles, and 12-byte ObjectIds.

[What to Say to Teacher and Class]:
"A common question is: 'Why not use MySQL or PostgreSQL?' 
In a mess, menus change every day. A meal contains between three to six dishes with variable portion 
sizes. In a relational database, you must normalize this across three tables with foreign keys and run 
costly SQL JOINs on every single read query.
In MongoDB, we applied the 'One-to-Few' document modeling principle: the menu items array is embedded 
directly inside the parent meal document. This gives us O(1) single-document reads with zero join latency, 
atomic updates, and direct JSON-to-Python interoperability. Furthermore, MongoDB's server-side aggregation 
engine computes all analytics natively in C++, saving network bandwidth and memory."

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[Visual Layout]:
- 4 Index Cards highlighting index types, keys, and execution stats (IXSCAN vs COLLSCAN).

[Slide Content]:
1. Unique Primary Index: `{"meal_id": 1}`
   - Guarantees zero duplicate ingestion of meal sessions.
2. Compound Unique Business Index: `[("meal_date", 1), ("meal_type", 1)]`

3. Single-Field B-Tree Index: `{"meal_date": 1}`
   - Converts collection scans (COLLSCAN) into index scans (IXSCAN) for historical time-series queries.
4. Multikey Array Index: `{"menu_items.item_name": 1}`
   - Indexes individual array elements, accelerating dish-specific waste searches across 1,500 documents.

[What to Say to Teacher and Class]:
"To ensure enterprise query performance, we engineered four specialized indexes in `database/indexes.py`. 
Most importantly, our compound unique index on `meal_date` and `meal_type` enforces a critical business 
rule: you cannot insert two Lunch records for the same date. 
Additionally, we built a Multikey Index on the embedded `menu_items.item_name` field. This indexes every 
individual dish within the embedded array, allowing instant lookups of dishes like 'Paneer Butter Masala' 
without doing a full collection scan."

PART 3: TOP 5 VIVA TRAP QUESTIONS (FACULTY DEFENSE CHEAT SHEET)
====================================================================================================

Q1: "Why is your R-squared score 0.4959? Why isn't it 0.90 or 0.95?"
Your Defense:
"Sir/Ma'am, in dining halls, diner behavior is inherently stochastic. Students leave unexpectedly or order 
outside food. Literature that reports R-squared above 0.90 almost always suffers from DATA LEAKAGE—they 
use post-meal actual attendance as an input feature. In an authentic, zero-leakage pre-meal operational 
setting, an R-squared of ~0.50 with an MAE of 14.2 kg is an outstanding, robust result that beats the baseline."

Q2: "Why didn't you normalize the menu items into a separate collection with foreign keys?"
Your Defense:
"In MongoDB, we follow the 1:Few document modeling rule. A meal has only 3 to 6 dishes. Normalizing them 
would require costly $lookup joins on every single read query. By embedding menu_items as a nested array, 
we achieve O(1) single-document reads with atomic writes and zero join latency."

Q3: "Can your Random Forest model output a negative leftover prediction?"
Your Defense:
"No, sir/ma'am. In `ml/predict.py`, we implement strict physical bounds:
y_pred = max(0.0, min(raw_prediction, total_prepared_kg)).
This mathematically guarantees the forecast is always non-negative and never exceeds food cooked."

Q4: "What is BSON and why is it preferred over JSON in MongoDB?"
Your Defense:
"BSON is Binary JSON. Standard JSON only supports strings, numbers, and booleans. BSON natively supports 
UTC Datetime, 64-bit IEEE floating-point doubles, and 12-byte ObjectIds. It also stores field length prefixes, 
allowing MongoDB to traverse documents rapidly without parsing every character."

Q5: "Why did you prioritize Recall over Precision in your High-Waste Classifier?"
Your Defense:
"A false positive means the kitchen prepares a slightly conservative buffer—minimal harm. But a false 
negative means a massive 50 kg surplus goes unnoticed and ends up in the dumpster. Therefore, we optimized 
Recall to 88.97% to guarantee 9 out of 10 severe waste spikes are flagged proactively."
